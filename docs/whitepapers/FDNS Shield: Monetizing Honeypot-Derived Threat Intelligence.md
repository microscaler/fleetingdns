# FDNS Shield: Monetizing Honeypot-Derived Threat Intelligence

## Introduction

Cyber attacks are accelerating in volume and sophistication, overwhelming enterprise security teams with alerts and malicious traffic. **Threat intelligence feeds** – streams of data about emerging attacks from external sources – have become vital for defenders to preempt threats. Honeypots, decoy systems that **lure attackers and log their behavior**, offer a rich source of such threat data. The logs of honeypot attacks provide essential cybersecurity intelligence that can be shared with analysts to give **relevant, contextual threat alerts**. This whitepaper introduces **FDNS Shield**, a SaaS product turning unique honeypot telemetry into a real-time defensive feed. Aimed at CISOs, enterprise security teams, and investors, we outline how FDNS Shield works, its technical architecture, competitive positioning, and the monetization strategy for this high-value data service. The global threat intelligence market is already sizable (projected \~\$6–13 B in 2025) and growing rapidly, making FDNS Shield not only a security innovation but a timely business opportunity.

## Honeypot Threat Data – An Untapped Resource

**FleetingDNS Honeypots** are specialized DNS-over-TLS endpoints configured with certificate pinning. They detect “pin mismatch” connections – a strong signal of illicit interception or scanning activity. Every time a malicious scanner or bot contacts these decoy domains with an unexpected TLS fingerprint or behavior, the honeypot captures detailed telemetry: the source IP address, the TLS handshake fingerprint (e.g. JA3 hash), geolocation and ASN, and behavioral metadata (e.g. request patterns, repetition rate). Over time, this generates a **wealth of data on hostile scanners and bots** targeting internet-facing systems. Rather than simply observing attacks in isolation, FDNS Shield aggregates this data to derive **actionable threat intelligence**. Each attacking IP can be enriched with context (e.g. known malware signature, anomalies in behavior) and given a **risk score** via machine learning. This transforms raw honeypot hits into a continuously updated **blocklist of “bad actor” IP addresses** with rich context. Organizations can consume this feed to automatically **shield their networks** from emerging threats, effectively turning the attackers’ activities against them. In essence, when one of our honeypots is hit, every FDNS Shield subscriber can be alerted and protected within seconds – a powerful network effect for collective defense.

## Solution Overview: FDNS Shield Feed

FDNS Shield is a cloud-based **threat intelligence feed service** that streams high-risk IP indicators in real time to enterprises’ security controls. The service – offered as **“Threat-Intel Streaming”** – takes the pin-mismatch honeypot telemetry and delivers **machine-scored, evidence-backed alerts** to customers’ firewalls and SIEMs. The goal is to monetize the unique data our honeypots collect by packaging it as a subscription feed called *FDNS Shield*. Key characteristics of the solution include:

* **Real-Time Blocking:** FDNS Shield pushes new malicious IP indicators to subscribers within **seconds** of detection, with an SLA of under 60 seconds from attack observation to customer update. This immediacy means customers can **block threats proactively** before attacks spread.
* **Machine-Learned Risk Scoring:** Every IP in the feed is scored 0–100 by an ML model for likelihood of maliciousness, so customers can prioritize or filter. Only high-confidence threats (e.g. score ≥80) are propagated by default, minimizing false positives.
* **Contextual Metadata:** Each feed record includes not just the IP, but **fingerprints and context** – e.g. TLS JA3 fingerprint, first-seen time, tags for threat type, and a link to evidence (detailed honeypot logs) for SOC analysis. Security teams get the “why” behind a block recommendation, aiding triage and trust in the feed.
* **Seamless Integrations:** The feed is delivered in formats easy to plug into defenses – via HTTPS **webhook pushes** or a persistent **gRPC stream**. Out-of-the-box integrations (scripts, connectors) are provided for popular platforms like Cloudflare WAF, Palo Alto **MineMeld** threat aggregator, AWS WAF, and others, so that **firewall rules can update automatically** with feed data.
* **Enterprise-Grade Delivery:** The service uses secure authentication (JWT + mTLS), supports backpressure (clients can acknowledge and control flow), and maintains high throughput (2,000 records/sec) to handle large-scale attacks. Reliability features like retry with exponential backoff (for webhooks) and historical replay on reconnect ensure no data is missed.
* **Compliance and Privacy:** Raw honeypot data is retained only short-term (30 days) and anonymized/aggregated data for one year, balancing intel value with privacy. GDPR deletion requests are honored with a defined SLA (e.g. erase a specific IP’s data within 30 days), and all data policies are enforced via configuration-as-code for transparency.

## Architecture and Workflow

&#x20;*Figure 1: High-level architecture of FDNS Shield. Honeypots capture attacker traffic (①), the cloud analytics pipeline scores and enriches threats (②, ③), and intelligence is streamed to client defenses (④) and SOC teams (④b). Compromised IPs are blocked at customer firewalls (⑤), preventing future attacks.*

The FDNS Shield system consists of several components working in concert (Figure 1):

* **Global Honeypot Network:** FleetingDNS honeypot instances are deployed across multiple locations. They masquerade as legitimate DNS-over-TLS services with pinned certificates. Attackers performing Man-in-the-Middle or scanning attacks trigger **TLS handshake anomalies**, which the honeypot logs. Each event includes source IP, JA3 TLS fingerprint, attempted domain, timestamp, and other signals. These honeypots act as an “early warning radar,” detecting malicious actors on the internet.
* **Ingestion & Data Pipeline:** Honeypot events stream into a cloud-based pipeline (built on Google Cloud). A Dataflow job continuously aggregates recent events per IP, computing features like frequency of hits, diversity of targets, geolocation, ASN reputation, and entropy of behavior. Events are written to BigQuery (for long-term analysis) and to a **Feature Store** that feeds the ML model. This pipeline is optimized for low latency – events move from edge sensors to the scoring engine within seconds.
* **ML Scoring Engine:** A **LightGBM machine learning model** is trained on months of honeypot data labeled with known malicious/benign outcomes (labels can be derived from cross-correlating with known threat lists or attack success indicators). This model scores each incoming IP on the likelihood of being a serious threat. For example, an IP hitting many honeypot domains with an unusual TLS fingerprint and high frequency might score 95 (very malicious). The model is tuned to achieve high recall (catch the vast majority of true attacks) while keeping false positives low (targeting >90% detection at <1% false alarm rate). It’s retrained weekly to adapt to new attacker tactics, with an automated pipeline using Vertex AI for model registry and deployment. Despite heavy analytics, the **online prediction service responds in <20ms**, ensuring scoring doesn’t add noticeable delay.
* **Threat Feed Service:** The core of FDNS Shield is the feed distribution layer. It exposes two primary interfaces for clients: (1) an **HTTPS Webhook** push – the service will `POST` new threat records to a customer-provided endpoint, signed with HMAC for integrity; and (2) a **gRPC bidirectional stream** (`ThreatFeed` service) where clients connect and receive a live stream of ThreatRecord messages (and can ACK receipt). Both methods carry the same data: a protobuf message containing the malicious IP, its risk score, threat tags (e.g. “scanner”, “botnet”, “MITM”), and an `evidence_url` linking to details (pcap logs or analysis) for that indicator. The feed service authenticates clients via JWT (each subscriber gets a token with their tenant ID) and enforces entitlements – e.g. if a subscription expires or is limited to certain data, the service will refuse or filter (HTTP 403 for unauthorized requests). The system is built in Rust (using the `tokio-tonic` framework for gRPC) to maximize performance and safety. Back-pressure signals from clients are honored (a slow client won’t be flooded – the server will buffer or wait as needed). Additionally, if a client disconnects, on reconnection they can request a **historical replay** to catch up on any missed records (ensuring continuity even if there was downtime). This robust design means the feed is **reliable and real-time**, with redundancy and security at its core (including TLS mutual authentication and cert pinning on both sides).
* **Integration Adapters:** To make the intelligence immediately usable, FDNS Shield provides integration scripts and tools. For example, a **Cloudflare Worker** script (in JavaScript) can subscribe to the gRPC feed and update Cloudflare’s edge firewall rules (via their API or Workers KV store) whenever a new IP arrives – thus propagating blocks globally in seconds. For Palo Alto networks, a prototype **MineMeld node** can ingest our webhook feed and publish it as a dynamic block list to Palo Alto firewalls. Terraform modules and documentation are provided for easy deployment of these integrations. The focus is on a **plug-and-play experience** – enterprise admins can get the feed connected to their existing security controls with minimal effort. We test integration performance to ensure adding these dynamic rules does not degrade devices (e.g. Cloudflare Workers processing under 10ms per update, firewall rule propagation under 30s) and we maintain these connectors as open source (Apache-2.0 license) to encourage transparency and community contributions.
* **Customer Portal & SOC Dashboard:** Subscribers access a portal to manage their **honeypot domain** and feed settings. Through the portal, a customer can register a new decoy domain (which we configure in our honeypots) unique to them if desired, set up their webhook URL and secret, and select what severity of alerts to receive. The portal also includes a dashboard where the security team can see real-time feed activity: e.g. how many indicators delivered today, top attacker IPs, and drill-down into each with the evidence and context. This serves as a SOC triage aid – when an alert comes in, analysts can quickly retrieve the background (e.g. “IP X was flagged due to TLS fingerprint matching known malware toolkit, first seen 2 days ago scanning 50 honeypot sites”). The evidence detail helps **justify blocks to management or auditors** by showing what the attacker attempted. Usage data (for billing) is also shown – e.g. number of indicators consumed in the month. The portal ties everything together with an intuitive UI for both technical and executive users, reinforcing the value of the feed with visual insights.

**End-to-End Workflow:** Figure 1 above and the sequence diagram below illustrate how an attack is detected and propagated through FDNS Shield to protect subscribers. In essence, one organization’s honeypot encounter can immediately safeguard all other members of the network.

&#x20;*Figure 2: Sequence diagram of FDNS Shield in action. When an attacker scans a decoy domain (1), the honeypot logs the attempt and forwards telemetry to the cloud (2). The analytics engine scores the threat and pushes a new block indicator to subscribers (3). If the attacker later targets a protected enterprise, that IP is already on the blocklist (4), and the firewall intercepts the traffic, effectively **blocking the attack**.*

This sequence demonstrates the **collaborative defense** enabled by FDNS Shield. An attack on one decoy instantly becomes an advantage for all subscribers: within <60 seconds, all customer firewalls worldwide can automatically block the offending IP. Security teams receive context-rich alerts at the same time, so they can verify and monitor (e.g. check if the IP attempted anything against their infrastructure). The result is a **dramatically shortened kill chain** – threats are identified and mitigated near-real-time, often before they even reach their intended targets. By leveraging our honeypot sensor network, enterprises no longer operate in isolation; they effectively share an early-warning system for emerging attacks.

## AI and ML: Enhancing Value with Intelligence

At the heart of FDNS Shield is a data-driven approach. The **machine learning model** not only filters noise but also provides adaptive intelligence that improves as it learns from new attacks. The use of ML means the feed isn’t a simple blacklist – it’s a smart list that adjusts to attacker behavior. For instance, novel attack patterns (e.g. a burst of distributed low-frequency scans) can be recognized by the model even if the IPs were never seen before, by their anomalous features. The high recall of the model ensures **even stealthy threats get identified**, while the low false positive rate means customers don’t waste time on innocuous events. This balance is crucial; as one industry comparison notes, traditional threat intel platforms offer comprehensive data analytics, whereas platforms like GreyNoise excel in filtering out non-relevant “noise” to reduce false positives. FDNS Shield is designed to offer the best of both: advanced analytics to catch real threats, and noise-reduction so that only **actionable alerts** go to security teams.

Looking ahead, FDNS Shield’s data pipeline opens the door to incorporating **Large Language Models (LLMs)** and advanced AI for even greater value. With a growing trove of attacker profiles and behaviors, we could train a domain-specific LLM on our corpus of threats. This AI assistant could, for example, automatically correlate an incoming malicious IP with known threat actor techniques or historical patterns, and produce a human-readable **threat profile report** for analysts. It might answer questions (“Is this IP associated with any known APT groups or malware campaigns?”) by cross-referencing internal and open intelligence sources, something an LLM can help summarize. LLMs can also help **enrich IOCs with context in natural language** and even suggest remediation steps. By embedding an LLM into the FDNS Shield portal, a CISO could ask in plain English, “What was the most common attack technique seen this month?” and get an insightful summary drawn from the data. Using AI in threat intelligence isn’t just hype – *it’s a game-changer*, helping analysts move from reactive to proactive by automating tedious correlation and surfacing insights. We plan to leverage private, fine-tuned LLMs (ensuring no sensitive data leaves our environment) to augment the FDNS Shield service. This will further differentiate our offering by turning raw data into **deep knowledge**, solidifying our product’s value as not just a feed, but an intelligent security advisor.

## Competitive Landscape

It’s important to understand FDNS Shield in context of existing solutions:

* **GreyNoise:** GreyNoise is perhaps the closest analog – it runs a large internet sensor network to collect **“Internet background noise”** and identifies which scanner IPs can be safely ignored or should be blocked. GreyNoise’s strength is filtering out common worms and scanners to help analysts focus. FDNS Shield, by contrast, is more focused on *targeted malicious behavior* (the TLS pin mismatch vector is a strong indicator of attempted mischief rather than benign scanning). Whereas GreyNoise provides an API for querying IP reputation and tags (and has a community edition), FDNS Shield emphasizes **real-time push** of threats as they unfold, and specific integration with firewall actions. We also provide richer per-indicator evidence (GreyNoise gives context like “known scanner” tags, but we offer actual handshake details and a trail of what the attacker attempted). In competitive positioning, FDNS Shield can be presented as a complementary feed – organizations might use GreyNoise to reduce noise in SIEM alerts and FDNS Shield to **actively block high-risk actors**. In terms of pricing, GreyNoise offers a SaaS model as well (including free limited use), so we position our pricing (discussed below) to be competitive given our additional real-time capabilities and unique data.
* **Anomali ThreatStream (and other TIPs):** ThreatStream is a Threat Intelligence Platform that aggregates feeds and allows enterprises to manage and analyze threat data. It’s a broader system (with integrations, case management, etc.), often used by mature SOCs to correlate multiple intel sources. FDNS Shield is not a TIP but rather a **feed provider** that could plug into such platforms. Our advantage is agility and focus: we produce a very timely, high-signal feed from our own telemetry. For a customer deciding between relying on generic feeds in a TIP vs. subscribing to FDNS Shield, we argue that our data will include novel indicators they won’t get elsewhere (especially concerning DNS-based attack attempts) and that we require minimal setup. If a client already has a TIP like Anomali, FDNS Shield can integrate into it (for instance, via STIX/TAXII or custom API ingestion). Compared to big intel vendors like Recorded Future or CrowdStrike’s feeds, our scope is narrower (we focus on network indicators like IPs and domains, not the full spectrum of intel like actor profiles or vulnerabilities). However, that focus means we excel at what we do – **real-time infrastructure threat blocking** – at a likely lower cost and complexity. FDNS Shield can thus be positioned as a **specialist feed** that complements broader intel solutions, or as a lightweight alternative for companies that need actionable data but don’t have resources for a full TIP.
* **Open Source and Free Feeds:** Many organizations use community feeds (like AbuseIPDB, Spamhaus, emerging threat lists, etc.). These are free or low-cost but often come with significant noise and slower update cycles. Our ML curation and <60s update SLA are key differentiators – we offer **fresher and more reliable data** with an enterprise support wrapper (SLA, integration help, etc.). While free feeds might update daily or hourly, FDNS Shield is **continuously streaming**. Over time, as our honeypot network grows, the sheer volume and uniqueness of data will further set us apart. We will, however, need to constantly demonstrate higher precision to justify the cost over free alternatives. Testimonials or case studies showing how we blocked an attack that evaded other feeds will be powerful sales tools.

In summary, FDNS Shield stands out by combining **original data collection, real-time delivery, and machine intelligence**. GreyNoise’s CEO has described their service as providing actionable perimeter threat intelligence without the noise – a statement we echo but with our own twist: FDNS Shield provides *actionable, real-time* threat intel with context that enterprises can **immediately act on**. We carve a niche alongside giants by being faster, smarter, and highly focused on the DNS attack surface which is often under-served in threat intel.

## Monetization Strategy: Tiers and Revenue Model

To turn this innovative feed into a sustainable business, FDNS Shield will employ a **tiered subscription model with usage-based billing**. The tiered approach allows us to target different customer segments – from smaller security teams to large enterprises – while the usage billing aligns revenue with the actual value delivered (e.g. volume of threats blocked). The tiers proposed are:

1. **Insight Tier** – *Entry-level offering for visibility.* Priced at approximately **\$500 per month** (or \~\$6k/year), this tier is ideal for small organizations or those just starting with threat intel feeds. Insight subscribers get real-time feed access but limited to a smaller scope: for example, they can register up to **1 feed endpoint** (one integration, e.g. their primary firewall), and the feed might include only **high-severity indicators** (risk score 90+). They receive summary analytics in the portal (trends and basic reports) but perhaps not the full evidence details. The latency SLA is the standard <60s, and they get community support (email/forum) rather than dedicated support. This tier’s goal is to provide immediate value (blocking the most obvious bad actors) at a low cost, and entice customers to upgrade once they see the benefits. It could also serve as a **trial tier** for 1-2 months before upgrading.

2. **Hunt Tier** – *Advanced offering for active threat hunting.* Priced around **\$1,500 per month** (\~\$18k/year), Hunt is aimed at mid-sized enterprise teams or MSSPs. It includes everything in Insight plus more: e.g. **up to 3 feed endpoints** (allowing multi-site or multi-platform integration, like one for cloud WAF, one for on-prem firewall, one for a SIEM). The feed provided is fuller – perhaps all indicators with score ≥80 (a broader feed for those who want more data). **ML score access** is included, meaning the customer can see the numeric risk score and additional metadata for each indicator, not just a binary “bad IP” flag. They also gain access to **advanced analytics** in the portal, such as the ability to search historical indicators or download monthly intel reports. This tier comes with standard support (business-hours SLA, integration assistance). The name “Hunt” implies that users can proactively hunt through our data – we might provide an API for querying the last 30 days of data, for example, so they can look up if an IP has ever hit our honeypots (useful for incident response). The Hunt tier is expected to be the most popular, balancing depth of intel with cost.

3. **Enterprise Tier** – *Premium offering for large organizations.* Priced at **\$5,000+ per month** (roughly \$60k/year and up, potentially negotiated based on scale), Enterprise tier is for companies that consider threat intel mission-critical (e.g. big banks, tech firms, or governments). There are **no hard limits** on feed endpoints – they can consume the feed in multiple ways (cloud, on-prem, multiple departments) globally. The SLA is enhanced: e.g. latency guarantee <30s and 24/7 dedicated support with faster response times. Enterprise customers get **full data access** – all scored indicators (even lower scores if they want to see them), longer data retention in the portal (access to 12+ months of history), and the evidence details for every record. They may also receive **custom integrations or onboarding assistance** as part of the package, and possibly the option to deploy a **private honeypot** instance if they want exclusive data (for example, we could deploy a decoy in their environment feeding into our cloud – giving them tailored intel). Billing for Enterprise can be more customized: a base subscription plus **overage charges** if they exceed a certain number of indicators per month. For example, the plan might include up to 100k indicators/month and then charge a small fee per additional 1k indicators to accommodate heavy attack periods. Enterprise tier cements our product as a partner in the client’s defense strategy, complete with compliance assurances and maybe even **joint threat research** opportunities.

**Usage-Based Billing:** Across all tiers, we measure “**billable events**” – likely defined as “number of IP indicators delivered with score ≥80” (as mentioned in our design). This ensures that if the threat landscape spikes (more attacks, hence more indicators), our revenue scales accordingly. For instance, if a Hunt tier customer normally gets 5,000 indicators/month included and a major outbreak causes 10,000 one month, the extra 5,000 could be billed at a rate (say \$0.01 per indicator, which would be \$50 extra). This usage billing is reported transparently in the portal’s usage graph and fed into our Stripe billing integration nightly. By keeping accuracy high, we ensure customers feel they are paying for true positives that provided protection, which is usually easy to justify in terms of ROI (e.g. “that feed blocked 3 ransomware bot IPs that could have caused a breach – easily saving us thousands or more”). The **metering accuracy** is kept within ±1% error and over-usage is throttled gracefully (we’d rather drop low-score indicators than overload and surprise-bill the customer, adhering to any contract limits).

**Revenue Projections:** Based on these tiers, we project a healthy growth trajectory. In the **first year**, assuming we convert \~50 customers (e.g. 30 on Hunt, 10 on Enterprise, 10 on Insight as a mix), annual recurring revenue (ARR) could reach on the order of **\$1–2 million**. This is plausible given the need in the market and the fact that even a handful of large enterprises at \~\$60k each contribute significantly. By year 2–3, with product maturity and a dedicated sales push, we aim for **hundreds of customers globally**. For example, with 200 customers (perhaps 100 Insight, 80 Hunt, 20 Enterprise as one scenario), the ARR would be in the **\$5–10 million** range. Due to the growing threat landscape, many organizations subscribe to multiple intel feeds; capturing just a small fraction of the addressable market can yield substantial revenue. Longer term, if we tap into even 5% of the global market for specialized threat feeds, that’s potentially thousands of enterprises – representing *eight-figure ARR potential* for FDNS Shield. Given the market’s projected growth to tens of billions of dollars in the coming decade, these estimates are conservative.

We will monitor our pricing model against usage to ensure we deliver clear value – if the feed prevents even one major incident, the cost pays for itself. This value proposition will drive sales conversations. We also anticipate **expanding revenue streams** by introducing the AI/LLM-driven premium features (which could be an add-on subscription for automated threat reports or a higher tier beyond Enterprise), and by offering professional services (e.g. custom threat research or integration consulting) to those who need it. These additional streams can contribute 10-20% on top of the core subscription revenue.
